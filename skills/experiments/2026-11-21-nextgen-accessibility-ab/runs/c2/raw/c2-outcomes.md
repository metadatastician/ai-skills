### F1. Easy Read turns a minimum gap into a schedule and drops the 24-hour maximum
- Where: `dosing-easy-read.html`, clock row: "Give your child 1 dose every 4 hours." Original: `dosing.html` warning box: "Leave at least 4 hours between doses. Do not give more than 4 doses in 24 hours." Also each table row: "Up to 4 times in 24 hours".
- Problem: The rule "at least 4 hours apart, at most 4 in 24 hours" has become "every 4 hours". That changes the instruction: a minimum interval is now a fixed timetable. The cap of 4 doses in 24 hours, a critical number, is gone, and the page does not say it was cut. Read literally, "every 4 hours" gives 6 doses in 24 hours. It also suggests regular dosing whether or not the child still needs it, where the original says "if needed" and "up to". Under the theory's outcome codes this item is changed, and the cap is dropped (silent). The version is Unfaithful and should be withheld.
- Who is affected: Readers who rely on the Easy Read version: parents and carers with learning disabilities, low literacy or limited English, and any carer who is tired or anxious at night. This page is the version most likely to be followed literally and without the full page.
- Impact: Safety. A carer who follows the Easy Read path faithfully could give 6 doses in 24 hours, which is above the stated maximum. Performance: the reader is misinformed, not just uninformed.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Withhold the Easy Read page until it is regenerated from the current original and checked. Declare "at least 4 hours between doses" and "no more than 4 doses in 24 hours" as critical instruction/number items, and keep both in plain words, for example: "Wait at least 4 hours before the next dose. Never give more than 4 doses in 1 day (24 hours)." Do not write a schedule.
- Evidence: The two sentences quoted above. Nothing else on the Easy Read page mentions a maximum.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Have a panel of Easy Read users, including parents with learning disabilities, answer after reading only the Easy Read page: "Your child had a dose at 8 in the morning. When is the earliest you can give the next one?" "What is the most doses you can give in one day?" "If your child seems better, do you still give the next dose?" Code each answer as correctly informed, uninformed or misinformed. Repeat with the corrected version. Disabled readers decide whether the reworded rule is intact.

### F2. Easy Read is built from a superseded original (chain fault) and contradicts the current page on babies
- Where: `dosing-easy-read.html`: "Easy Read. Made on 14 March 2026." and "If your baby is under 3 months old, talk to a doctor first." `dosing.html`: "Page last reviewed: 3 November 2026", "under 2 months old ... unless it is after vaccinations", the "2 to 3 months (after vaccinations)" row, and the update note: "Earlier versions of this page said to speak to a doctor for any baby under 3 months."
- Problem: The Easy Read version was made almost 8 months before the 3 November 2026 correction, and it repeats the advice that the correction withdrew. The full page links to it with no warning, so the trust's two versions give different advice for babies aged 2 to 3 months. The correction notice on the full page is not carried into the Easy Read version (correction is a must-survive type). This is a principle 1 chain fault.
- Who is affected: Carers of babies aged 2 to 3 months who have just had vaccinations, using the Easy Read version, and anyone who compares the two versions and sees them disagree.
- Impact: Performance and comfort, more than direct overdose risk. The stale advice is the more cautious one, so it is more likely to send a carer to a doctor unnecessarily, or delay pain relief after vaccinations, than to cause harm. It also leaves the carer with two conflicting official answers and no way of telling which is current. It is not an overdose risk in itself, but see F1: once the doctor is consulted, the Easy Read version gives no 2-dose limit for this age.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Regenerate every adapted version from the current original whenever the original changes. Record the source version or date on the adapted page, for example "Made from the full page dated 3 November 2026". Make a correction to the original automatically mark or withdraw its dependent versions until they are remade. Carry the correction into the Easy Read text.
- Evidence: The dates and the update note quoted above.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Ask panel readers, Easy Read page only: "Your baby is 10 weeks old and had vaccinations today. Can you give paracetamol without asking a doctor first? How much? How many times?" Then ask readers who have seen both pages: "Which page do you trust for this answer, and why?"

### F3. Easy Read drops the medicine strength and the dose table, and sends the reader to the box
- Where: `dosing-easy-read.html`: "Look at the box to see how much to give." `dosing.html`: "Use the 120mg in 5ml liquid (infant suspension) for children under 6", plus the age and dose table (2.5ml / 5ml / 7.5ml / 10ml).
- Problem: The strength instruction is a critical item, and it is dropped without saying so. Every dose number is also dropped. The work of finding the dose moves to the packaging, which is usually denser and smaller print than the full page. Nothing tells the reader that liquid paracetamol comes in more than one strength, so a carer holding a higher-strength bottle gets no prompt that the dose differs.
- Who is affected: The Easy Read audience above, particularly anyone who cannot read small print on packaging and anyone with more than one paracetamol product at home.
- Impact: Safety: there is a risk of using the wrong strength, or reading a dose off the wrong product. Comfort: it creates undue effort by moving the reader from a supported format to an unsupported one. Performance: the reader cannot answer "how much?" from this version.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Keep the strength in plain words ("Use the medicine for babies and young children. The box says 120mg in 5ml."). Give the dose by age in an Easy Read table or one row per age, with a picture of the syringe filled to the mark. If the dose is deliberately left out, name the cut and say why.
- Evidence: The quoted lines. No number of millilitres appears anywhere on the Easy Read page.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Give panel readers the Easy Read page and two bottles of different strengths: "Which bottle is right for a 3-year-old? How much do you give?" Record correct, unable to answer, or wrong answer. Test whether a picture of a filled syringe helps or misleads. That is for the readers to settle.

### F4. Easy Read silently drops the warning about other medicines that contain paracetamol
- Where: `dosing.html` warning box: "Do not give paracetamol with other medicines that contain paracetamol, such as some cold and flu remedies." It does not appear in `dosing-easy-read.html`.
- Problem: A critical safety instruction is dropped without saying so. This is the main route to accidental double dosing.
- Who is affected: Easy Read users who also buy cold and flu remedies for children.
- Impact: Safety: the reader is uninformed about a common cause of overdose. Combined with F1, the total dose could be well above the maximum.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Add an Easy Read row, for example: "Some cold and flu medicines have paracetamol in them too. Do not give them with this medicine. Ask a pharmacist if you are not sure." Declare the warning as critical.
- Evidence: Absent from the Easy Read text.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Ask the panel: "Your child is taking this paracetamol. Can you also give a cold and flu syrup?" Code the answers as correctly informed, uninformed or misinformed.

### F5. Easy Read swaps the overdose emergency instruction for a general "talk to a doctor if worried"
- Where: `dosing.html`: "Call 999 or go to A&E if you think your child has taken too much paracetamol, even if they seem well." `dosing-easy-read.html`: "If you are worried, talk to a doctor."
- Problem: The emergency instruction, with its urgency and the warning "even if they seem well", is dropped. In its place is a weaker general instruction. "Even if they seem well" is a critical caveat, because paracetamol overdose may not show symptoms at first. A reader who only has the Easy Read version, and whose child seems well after too much, has no reason to act urgently.
- Who is affected: Easy Read users after a suspected overdose, which is the situation F1 and F4 make more likely.
- Impact: Safety: the response could be delayed or not happen at all. Performance: the reader does not know about 999 or A&E for this situation.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Keep the instruction in plain words with an emergency symbol: "If your child had too much paracetamol, call 999 or go to A&E now. Do this even if your child seems OK." Declare it as a critical instruction, with "even if they seem well" as a critical caveat.
- Evidence: The quoted lines.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Ask the panel: "You think your child had too much paracetamol, but they are playing happily. What do you do, and how quickly?"

### F6. Easy Read drops the 3-day fever threshold
- Where: `dosing.html`: "Speak to a doctor if your child still has a fever after 3 days". The Easy Read version has only "If you are worried, talk to a doctor."
- Problem: An important number (3 days) is dropped, and the cut is not named.
- Who is affected: Easy Read users caring for a child with a fever that goes on for days.
- Impact: Performance: the reader is not told when to escalate. The safety effect is moderate: it relies on the carer deciding they are worried.
- Severity: 1
- Fix: Add "If your child still has a high temperature after 3 days, talk to a doctor." Alternatively, name the cut.
- Evidence: Absent from the Easy Read text.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Ask: "Your child has had a temperature for 4 days but is drinking. Should you talk to a doctor?"

### F7. The Easy Read version carries no fidelity label, no machine-made mark and no source record
- Where: `dosing-easy-read.html` header: "Easy Read. Made on 14 March 2026." `dosing.html` link: "Easy Read version".
- Problem: The case says an AI writing tool made the page, but the page does not say so. It does not state which version of the original it was made from, whether anyone checked it, or what was left out. Its status is Unchecked at best, and on F1 to F5 it is Unfaithful, yet both the link and the page present it as an equivalent version of the guidance. Under the theory, an EU AI Act Article 50 mark would be due for such a rewrite (legal point: ASSUMED). That mark alone would still not tell the reader what changed.
- Who is affected: Every reader of the Easy Read version, and carers who send it on to others.
- Impact: Performance and safety: readers have no signal that this version is incomplete for dosing, so they rely on it in place of the full page.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Withhold the page until it is checked (F1 to F5). Once remade, label it with who or what made it, the source date, its check status, and a plain list of anything left out, for example: "This Easy Read page does not have the full dose table. Ask a pharmacist." Put the same status on the link from the full page.
- Evidence: The page header and link text quoted above. The case statement says the pages are made with an AI writing tool. Whether labels improve reader outcomes is an OPEN claim.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether readers notice and understand a status line, and whether it changes which version they rely on. Disabled readers decide what wording of the label counts as clear.

### F8. Full page: what to do for babies under 3 months is ambiguous
- Where: `dosing.html`, "Before you start": "under 2 months old ... unless it is after vaccinations". Table row: "2 to 3 months (after vaccinations) ... One dose, then a second dose after 4 to 6 hours if needed. No more than 2 doses."
- Problem: The page gives three readings that do not fit together. (a) The bullet's "unless it is after vaccinations" can be read as permission to give paracetamol to a baby under 2 months after vaccinations, but the table has no row or dose below 2 months. (b) The page does not say what to do for a baby aged 2 to 3 months who has not had vaccinations. A reader may fall back on the 3 to 6 month row ("up to 4 times in 24 hours"). (c) "No more than 2 doses" has no time window. It could mean 2 doses in total, per day, or per vaccination. The 4 to 6 hour gap in this row also differs from the general "at least 4 hours" rule. I am not saying what the correct clinical advice is. The ambiguity is in the text.
- Who is affected: Carers of babies under 3 months using the full page, including screen-reader users moving through the table cell by cell, who hear the row header and the "How often" cell separately from the bullet above.
- Impact: Safety: a reader could give a dose to a baby the guidance did not cover, or give more doses than intended. Performance: an informed reader still cannot tell what to do in case (b).
- Severity: 2
- Fix: The trust's clinical authors should resolve this at source, then regenerate the derived versions. Say explicitly what to do for under 2 months (with and without vaccinations) and for 2 to 3 months without vaccinations. Give "No more than 2 doses" a time frame, for example "in total for this vaccination". Declare these as critical items.
- Evidence: The quoted lines. Read together, they leave cases (a) and (b) uncovered.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Ask a mixed panel, including screen-reader users reading the table: "Your baby is 7 weeks old and had vaccinations today. What does the page tell you to do?" "Your baby is 10 weeks old with a temperature, with no vaccinations. What does the page tell you to do?" "After a vaccination, how many doses can a 10-week-old have in total?"

### F9. Easy Read symbols may reinforce the wrong reading, and how well they work is untested
- Where: `dosing-easy-read.html`: images `sym/clock.png`, `sym/bottle.png` and others, all with `alt=""`.
- Problem: Each symbol is paired with one sentence. A clock next to "every 4 hours" may strengthen the schedule reading in F1, and the bottle sits next to the instruction that moves the reader away to the packaging. The symbols are hidden from assistive technology, so screen-reader users get the text alone, which is acceptable only if the text is correct. I cannot judge from the code whether the symbols help or mislead.
- Who is affected: Easy Read users who rely on the pictures more than the words.
- Impact: Uncertain. Comprehension could be helped or harmed. This is not a WCAG point.
- Severity: 1
- Fix: Choose symbols for the corrected sentences (for example, a clock showing a gap, or a "maximum 4" counter), and test them with users.
- Evidence: The pairing of image and text in the markup. The effect is not established.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Show the symbol rows with and without text, and ask what each picture tells the reader to do. Only Easy Read users can settle whether the symbols are intact.
