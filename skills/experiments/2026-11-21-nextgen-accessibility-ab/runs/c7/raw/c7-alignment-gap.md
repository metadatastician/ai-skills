### F1. The AI summary reverses the debate's outcome and the speakers' positions
- Where: `lecture.html`, `section.ai`, first paragraph ("Both speakers agreed that the four-day school week has failed and should be scrapped.")
- Problem: The transcript and captions (04:40) have Lindqvist saying "I disagree that it should be scrapped" and arguing to "reform it". The chair says "not agreement, but some common ground on secondary schools". The summary turns a disagreement into agreement. Under the outcome codes, Lindqvist's position is changed and misattributed, and Okafor's partial concession on secondary schools and Lindqvist's reform proposal are dropped silently. Any one of these makes the class Unfaithful. The page carries no must-survive declaration (principle 2), so I inferred the items from the transcript. The author has not weighted them.
- Who is affected: Readers who take the "gist" route instead of a 58-minute video or a long transcript. The section is offered for exactly that. They include people with cognitive or learning disabilities, people who tire easily, screen-reader users who meet the summary before the collapsed transcript, and deaf readers who would otherwise depend on captions.
- Impact: Readers come away misinformed about the central fact of the event, who held which view. Nothing on the page tells them the summary conflicts with the transcript beneath it.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Withhold the summary (class Unfaithful, label Withheld) until a version made from the edited transcript is checked against an author declaration. That declaration should mark each speaker's position, Okafor's secondary-school concession and the chair's "not agreement" as critical attribution and fact-or-opinion items.
- Evidence: Summary paragraph 1 compared with transcript paragraphs 3 to 5 and caption cues 00:04:40 to 00:04:53.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether readers who use summaries can tell, from the page as shipped, that the summary contradicts the transcript, and what disabled readers count as "intact" for a debate summary (principle 5).

### F2. The 12% figure is widened, loses its hedge and is attributed to both speakers
- Where: `lecture.html`, AI summary: "Dr Okafor and Professor Lindqvist cited falling attainment, with test scores down 12%"
- Problem: In the source, Okafor said "reading scores for the youngest pupils are down by about 12 per cent" (cue 00:03:16.500). The summary changes the scope from reading scores for the youngest pupils to "test scores". It drops the hedge "about" and attributes the figure to both speakers. Lindqvist accepted the reading figure but said "maths scores are flat", which is the opposite of general "falling attainment". This is a number item changed, a hedge item dropped and an attribution item misattributed.
- Who is affected: Every reader of the summary. Most of all, readers who rely on short text and do not cross-check against the long transcript.
- Impact: A precise, hedged claim by one speaker becomes a broad, certain claim both speakers endorse.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: The same as F1. A checked version must keep the exact figure, its scope, its hedge and its speaker.
- Evidence: Caption cues 00:03:16.500 and 00:04:44.000, and transcript paragraphs 2 and 3.
- Needs disabled readers: no

### F3. The summary invents a factual claim about policy reversal and gives it to the chair

- Severity: 3

- Evidence: Last summary sentence compared with transcript paragraph 5. Childcare sentence compared with transcript paragraph 2.
- Needs disabled readers: no

### F4. The summary was made from the automatic captions, not the edited original (chain fault)
- Where: `lecture.html`, `p.meta`: "Generated by StreamScribe from the automatic captions on 3 October 2026", next to "Captions edited ... on 5 October 2026".
- Problem: The summary comes from a superseded, uncorrected version and was never regenerated from the edited captions or the transcript. This breaks principle 1, and loss along that chain cannot be recovered downstream. The page shows both dates, but nothing tells readers the summary predates the corrected source.
- Who is affected: Summary readers. The page does not let me establish whether the automatic captions also carried the errors that deaf readers would have met before 5 October.
- Impact: Errors in the automatic captions may carry into the summary. I cannot tell from the page whether F1 to F3 come from the captions or the summariser.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Make every version from the edited transcript. Record its source and date, and invalidate a version whenever its source is corrected.
- Evidence: The two dates and source statements in `lecture.html`.
- Needs disabled readers: no

### F5. The provenance line says a machine was involved but not that nobody checked it or what changed
- Where: `lecture.html`, `section.ai` heading "AI summary" and `p.meta`.
- Problem: "AI summary" and "Generated by StreamScribe" meet a machine-made mark of the EU AI Act Article 50 kind. They carry no Unchecked label, no statement of what was cut, and no warning that the summary may disagree with the transcript. The provenance comes after the summary text in reading order and is set smaller and greyer, so screen-reader and sighted users meet the claims before the caveat. The intro sentence ("for readers who want the gist", from the case brief) and the box's placement above the transcript present it as a dependable shortcut.
- Who is affected: People who stop reading after the summary. This includes cognitive-access users and screen-reader users who move on by heading.
- Impact: The mark passes as disclosure while giving readers no basis to judge whether to trust the summary.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Put the label before the summary text, for example "Unchecked machine summary: may contain errors; the transcript below is the record". After a check, show its label and what was cut. If the summary is withheld, say so.
- Evidence: DOM order inside `section.ai`, and the `.ai p.meta` styles.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether a label placed before or after the summary changes whether readers notice it and how far they rely on the summary.

### F6. The unchecked summary is the prominent "gist" route while the authoritative transcript is collapsed
- Where: `lecture.html`. `section.ai` is a named region (`aria-labelledby`) with an h2, placed before `<details><summary>Full transcript</summary>`, which has no heading.
- Problem: The markup makes the summary easy to reach. A labelled landmark with a heading puts it in the landmark and heading lists, while the transcript has no heading and starts collapsed. Automated checks pass both. The structure sends heading and landmark navigators, and readers who want less effort, to the least faithful version. To my knowledge, collapsed `<details>` content stays in the DOM, so software that reads the markup gets the transcript as easily as the summary. Burdening people more than software is an alignment-gap effect, and that rests on a DESIGNED, untested claim.
- Who is affected: Screen-reader users navigating by heading or landmark, cognitive-access users and magnifier users, who see the boxed summary first.
- Impact: The shortest path to the content leads to the distorted version.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Give the transcript its own heading, for example h2 "Full transcript", and consider expanding it or linking to it from the top. Make the faithful record at least as easy to find as any adaptation.
- Evidence: Heading structure and order in `lecture.html`.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test which route people take to "the gist", and whether a collapsed transcript with no heading is found by heading navigation.

### F7. Speaker identification in captions relies on voice tags that may not be shown on screen
- Where: `captions-excerpt.vtt`, `<v Amara Okafor>` and `<v Nils Lindqvist>` cues.
- Problem: Speaker names appear only as WebVTT voice spans and not in the cue text. Native browser caption rendering does not reliably display voice names, so the names may be machine-readable but invisible to viewers. The cue at 00:04:40 changes speaker with no visible marker if the name is not rendered. What is seen may then differ from what the file "says". I cannot verify rendering from the material.
- Who is affected: Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers using the captions, especially where speakers are off-camera or the shot does not show who is speaking.
- Impact: In a debate, misattributing who said "should be scrapped" against "I disagree" is the same failure as F1, reached by a different route.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Check rendering in the target player and browsers. If names are not shown, add visible speaker labels at each speaker change, for example "AMARA OKAFOR:" or "[Nils Lindqvist]".
- Evidence: Cue text in `captions-excerpt.vtt`. Rendering behaviour is not tested here.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether caption users can tell who is speaking at each change in the actual player.

### F9. An agent-oriented fix could spread the distortion
- Where: `lecture.html`, `section.ai` (risk, not a present defect)
- Problem: The page has no structured data. If an agent-readiness change reused the existing summary as the video's machine description, for example as a schema.org `description` or a search snippet, or presented it as the page's abstract, the Unfaithful summary would reach readers through search results, assistants and read-aloud tools, away from the transcript that corrects it. This is the reverse direction: a fix aimed at software would harm people. Readers outside the page could not check it against the transcript at all.
- Who is affected: Anyone who meets the debate through an assistant or search instead of the page, including people who use assistants because the page or video is hard for them.
- Impact: Misinformation spreads without the transcript beside it.
- Severity: 1
- Fix: Never use an unchecked or withheld version as machine-facing metadata. Take any machine description from the author's declaration or a Checked version.
- Evidence: Absence of structured data in `lecture.html`, combined with the summary's content (F1 to F3).
- Needs disabled readers: no

Not found: structured data (JSON-LD, microdata), hidden text for agents or search, keyword-stuffed or verbose accessible names, ARIA added for "AI readiness", live regions, generated alt text, automated accessibility scores, badges or conformance claims on the page, a must-survive declaration from the author, any label other than "AI summary" (Exact, Checked, Unchecked or Withheld), and anything that sends readers' needs or preferences to the publisher.
