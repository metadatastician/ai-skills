### F1. Every short path gives the corrected, wrong cost (£4.8m)
- Where: Key points item 1; `<meta name="description">`, which is also sent to news aggregators; "Listen to this article", which reads the key points before the body.
- Problem: The approved cost is £4.2m. The correction of 14 November withdraws £4.8m. The key points and the meta description still say £4.8m, so they were made from the earlier original or never regenerated after the correction. This is a chain fault under principle 1. Coded against the article, the number is changed, so the version is Unfaithful and should be withheld.
- Who is affected: Anyone who reads only the summary. That includes aggregator and search readers, who may never see the page, and readers who use summaries to cut reading load, such as people with cognitive or learning disabilities, fatigue or low literacy. It also includes screen-reader and Listen users, who meet £4.8m before they meet £4.2m. The aggregator snippet is the version most likely to be relied on, because for many readers it is the only one they receive.
- Impact: Performance: the reader is misinformed about the headline fact of a public spending decision. Safety: the wrong number can be repeated, quoted or used to judge the council. On the full page and in audio, the reader hears £4.2m (headline), then £4.8m (key points), then £4.2m (body), and the correction only at the very end. Settling the contradiction takes effort the reader should not have to spend (comfort).
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Regenerate the key points and the meta description from the current, corrected original. Any correction should trigger regeneration, and the old summary should be withdrawn until the new one is checked. Declare the cost as a critical number. Push the corrected description to aggregators. Outcome test, separately for each path (aggregator snippet only, key points only, Listen, full page): "How much will the barrier cost?" "Has this figure ever been corrected? If so, from what to what?"
- Evidence: Meta description and key points item 1 say "£4.8m". The `<title>`, `<h1>` and paragraph 1 say "£4.2m". The correction reads: "an earlier version of this article said the scheme would cost £4.8m. The approved cost is £4.2m."
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether readers who rely on the key points or on Listen come away with the correct cost, and whether they notice and can resolve the conflicting figures.

### F2. Opposition quote is attributed to the councillor who backs the scheme
- Where: Key points item 2; the meta description and aggregator feed.
- Problem: "Rushed through without consultation" was said by Malik Osei, chair of the Lower Wendham Residents' Association. The key points give it to Councillor Ana Ruiz, the cabinet member who supports the scheme. The meta description goes further: it merges her support quote and the opponent's quote into one speaker ("Councillor Ana Ruiz said, adding that…"). These are a misattribution and a merge. Both are distortions, so the version is Unfaithful whatever the weight.
- Who is affected: Readers of the key points, the snippet and the audio. Residents trying to work out who holds which position, and people deciding whether or how to respond. Ruiz and Osei themselves are misrepresented. On aggregators, readers cannot check the claim against the article.
- Impact: Performance: the reader cannot follow the argument; the main conflict disappears, and the decision-maker appears to condemn her own scheme. Safety: the reader is misinformed about a named public official's position, which is a reputational and right-of-reply harm. Osei's side vanishes.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Declare attributions and direct quotes as critical, and keep speaker and quote paired. Regenerate, and withhold any version that changes who said what. Outcome test: "Who said the scheme was rushed through without consultation?" "What is Councillor Ruiz's position on the barrier?" "Who opposes it, and why?"
- Evidence: Key points: "Councillor Ana Ruiz said the scheme had been rushed through without consultation." Article: Malik Osei, chair of the Residents' Association: "This has been rushed through without proper consultation." Meta: "…900 homes in Lower Wendham, Councillor Ana Ruiz said, adding that the scheme had been rushed through without consultation."
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test who readers of each path believe holds each position.

### F3. Hedges dropped: "will protect" replaces "expect … cut the risk … cannot remove it"
- Where: Key points item 1; meta description.
- Problem: Ruiz's quote is hedged twice: "We expect this to cut the flood risk for around 900 homes, though no barrier can remove the risk entirely." The Environment Agency's statement is hedged too: the barrier "could reduce" the chance of flooding, and its modelling "was still being reviewed". The summary says it "will protect 900 homes", which drops every hedge and turns "around 900" into a firm 900. This is a changed hedge and a changed number, so the version is Unfaithful.
- Who is affected: Lower Wendham residents who read only the summary or the snippet, including people who depend on summaries to manage reading load. People whose own flood preparation is harder or slower are the most exposed to a false sense of safety, but how much they rely on it is for them to say.
- Impact: Safety: a reader could decide that flood risk is solved, and act on that (insurance, flood kit, sign-up for warnings, evacuation planning). Performance: the reader misses that the official modelling is unsettled.
- Severity: 3
- Fix: Declare both hedges (Ruiz's, and the Environment Agency's "could reduce" and "modelling still being reviewed") as critical caveats, and declare "around 900" as a number with its qualifier. Outcome test: "After the barrier is built, could homes in Lower Wendham still flood?" "How sure are the council and the Environment Agency about the effect?" "How many homes, roughly?"
- Evidence: Key points: "A £4.8m flood barrier will protect 900 homes." Body: "We expect this to cut the flood risk for around 900 homes, though no barrier can remove the risk entirely." EA: "could reduce", "modelling was still being reviewed".
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether readers relying on the summary or Listen keep the understanding that residual flood risk remains.

### F4. The January consultation is missing, and the summary implies there will be none
- Where: Key points; meta description.
- Problem: The body says a public consultation on the design opens in January, before construction. That is the one thing a reader can act on. No short version mentions it. Instead, the key points state, in a misattributed form, that there was no consultation, which leaves the impression that residents have no say. The item is silently dropped, and the remaining summary points the reader the wrong way.
- Who is affected: Residents who would respond to the consultation, especially those on Mill Lane, who stand to lose river access. Readers who use only the summary or the snippet.
- Impact: Performance: the reader cannot respond to the consultation and does not know one exists. Safety: the reader acts on a wrong picture (no chance to influence the design) and may not take part.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Declare the consultation (what it covers, that it opens in January, that it comes before construction) as a critical instruction item, and link to how to take part when that is known. Outcome test: "Is there a chance to comment on the barrier? When? On what?"
- Evidence: Body: "The council said a public consultation on the barrier's design would open in January, before construction begins." It is absent from the key points and the meta description.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether readers on each path know a consultation exists and when it opens, and whether they could take part.

### F5. Timeline turned from conditional to certain
- Where: Key points item 3.
- Problem: The body says work is "expected to start in spring 2027, subject to the consultation, with completion planned for late 2028". The key points say "Work starts in spring and the barrier will be finished by 2028". That drops the condition, the hedging ("expected", "planned") and the year of the start. It also shifts the end date from "late 2028" to "by 2028", which reads as earlier. These are changed items.
- Who is affected: Readers planning around the works, such as residents on and near Mill Lane, and readers of the summary or audio. "Spring" with no year is ambiguous when read in November 2026.
- Impact: Safety: a wrong deadline. A reader may expect works in spring 2026 or 2027 regardless of the consultation, and completion before late 2028. Performance: the reader misses that the consultation can change the schedule.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Declare the dates, and the condition "subject to the consultation", as critical. Keep the years and the hedges. Outcome test: "When is work expected to start, and what could change that?" "When is it planned to finish?"
- Evidence: Key points: "Work starts in spring and the barrier will be finished by 2028." Body: "expected to start in spring 2027, subject to the consultation, with completion planned for late 2028."
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test the dates readers report from each path.

### F6. The correction and the right of reply do not reach the short versions or aggregators
- Where: Meta description and aggregator feed; key points; the reading order of the full page and of Listen.
- Problem: The correction exists only at the foot of the article. Aggregator readers, who receive only the meta description, get the uncorrected figure and no correction. Readers of the full page, screen readers and Listen meet the correction last, after a long passage. The contractor's response ("in due course") and the Mill Lane loss of river access are dropped silently. Both are at least important items; the contractor's line is a right of reply.
- Who is affected: Aggregator and search readers, who are the most exposed because the snippet is all they get. Listen and screen-reader users, who must get through the whole article to reach the correction. Readers who stop after the key points. Mill Lane residents.
- Impact: Performance: readers never learn the story was corrected, or who is affected and how. Comfort: a correction placed last makes readers hold a contradiction through the whole article.
- Severity: 2

Put a short correction notice next to the corrected figure, or near the top, as well as at the foot. Name any important cuts in the summary ("Not included: contractor's response; impact on Mill Lane river access"). Outcome test: "Has anything in this story been corrected?" "Who loses something under this scheme?" "Has the contractor responded?"
- Evidence: The meta description has no correction. The correction paragraph is the last element of `<article>`. The key points omit the Mill Lane item and the Hallam Civils item.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether listeners and screen-reader users report the correction, and how much effort reaching it takes.

### F7. The summary's label says it is made by a machine but not that it is unchecked, nor from what or when, and the label comes after the content
- Where: `p.note` in the key points box; the meta description and aggregator copy, which have no label at all.
- Problem: "Key points are generated automatically to help you read faster" partly marks the summary as machine-made, but it does not say Unchecked. It does not record what it was made from (which version of the article) or when. It frames the summary as a faithful speed aid. It sits after the list, so in linear reading (screen readers, Listen, keyboard users) the reader takes in the claims before learning their status. The aggregator copy carries no mark at all.
- Who is affected: Readers who trust the box because it is at the top and looks editorial, and readers in linear reading order. Whether labels improve outcomes is OPEN, so this finding rests on the label being a missing honesty duty, not on a proven outcome gain.
- Impact: Performance and safety: readers have no cue to distrust a version that is, in fact, Unfaithful (F1 to F5).
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Withhold the current key points (Unfaithful). If they return after a check, label them Checked or Unchecked, with the source version and date ("Made automatically from the article as updated 14 November 2026; not checked by an editor"), and put the label before the list. Do not award a label through the tool itself. Carry the mark into the meta description and aggregator payload. Outcome test: "Who or what wrote the key points? Were they checked? Which should you trust if they differ from the article?"
- Evidence: `<p class="note">` follows the `<ul>`. It has no version, date or check status. The meta tag has no mark.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test whether a label, and where it sits, changes what readers believe and rely on. The effect is OPEN.

### F8. Listen reads the faulty summary first and gives the listener little control
- Where: The `#listen` button and its script, which read `article.innerText`.
- Problem: The audio reads everything in `<article>` in order: the headline (£4.2m), the byline, the button label, "Key points", the three faulty points (£4.8m, the misattribution, certain dates), the note, the body, and finally the correction. The listener gets the Unfaithful version as part of what is labelled "this article", with no audible boundary between machine summary and journalism. There is no pause and resume, no control over rate or position, and no way to skip to the correction. The `aria-pressed` state is not reset when speech ends, so the button can report playing after the speech has stopped. Where a quote begins and ends may not be audible in synthetic speech, which risks blurring Ruiz's quote with the Environment Agency's paraphrase; that needs testing.
- Who is affected: Listen users: blind and partially sighted readers, people with dyslexia or other print disabilities, and people with fatigue. For them the audio may be the version relied on.
- Impact: Performance and safety: the listener hears contradictory figures and a misattributed quote before the facts. Comfort: there is no way to re-hear one passage or jump to the correction without restarting, and attention has to be held through the whole piece; pace is not set by the reader (principle 4).
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Exclude the summary from the read-aloud, or announce it as a separate, labelled, machine-made part, and make it skippable. Add pause and resume, rate control, and section skip, all set on the reader's side without reporting the choice to the site. Reset the button state on `end` and on `error`. Voice quote boundaries ("quote … end quote") if testing shows they are lost. Outcome test after listening only: "How much does the barrier cost?" "Who said it was rushed?" "Was anything corrected?" "Where did the quote from Councillor Ruiz end?"
- Evidence: `document.querySelector('article').innerText` (the key points section is inside `<article>`). There is no `onend` handler; there are only start and cancel.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Test comprehension from audio alone, whether quote boundaries are heard, and whether the controls make re-listening reasonable.

### F9. A panel test is needed to measure outcomes across all paths
- Where: Every path: the full page, key points, meta description and aggregator snippet, Listen, and screen-reader reading order.
- Problem: Nothing on the site measures whether readers end up correctly informed. A markup audit would pass most of this page, which has a skip link, landmarks, a labelled section and visible focus, while the summary misinforms.
- Who is affected: All readers. The most exposed are those who rely on the summary, the snippet or the audio.
- Impact: Without measurement, the failures in F1 to F8 stay invisible to the publisher.
- Severity: 2
- Fix: Run reader panels with one path per group and code each answer as correctly informed, uninformed or misinformed. Questions: (1) What did the council decide? (2) How much will it cost? (3) Roughly how many homes are affected, and is the flood risk removed? (4) Who supports the scheme, who opposes it, and why? (5) Is there a consultation, and when? (6) When will work start and finish, and on what does that depend? (7) Who loses something? (8) Was anything in the story corrected? (9) Which parts were written by a machine? Also record time taken and whether the reader had to go back over the material.
- Evidence: Not run. No outcome data is included in the material.
- Needs disabled readers: yes. Disabled readers should help decide what "intact" means for the summary and the audio (principle 5), and should be part of each path panel.
